# Advanced Audio Analysis Techniques for This Visualizer

## Purpose

This note explains how the current audio analysis works, why it is shaped this
way, and how the shader-facing data should be interpreted for musical visual
control.

The main problem is not that the FFT is "wrong". The problem is that a raw FFT
frame is too literal. It describes the spectrum at one instant, while a person
hears rhythm, phrase energy, instrumentation, brightness, density, harmony, and
section changes over time. If we feed shaders only single-frame magnitude data,
the visuals twitch and oscillate because every small spectral change becomes a
visual event.

The goal is to turn sound into stable musical control signals:

- Fast signals for hits: kick, snare, hats, transient spikes.
- Medium signals for groove: beat envelope, beat phase, onset density.
- Slow signals for shape: buildup, break, drop return, layer changes.
- Timbre signals for look: brightness, noisiness, percussive/harmonic balance,
  spectral contrast.
- Harmony signals for color: chroma/pitch-class energy and harmonic motion.

This matches the broad feature split used in music information retrieval (MIR):
timbral texture, rhythmic content, and pitch/harmonic content.


## What The Current Analyzer Does

The implementation lives in src/AudioAnalysis.h and src/AudioAnalysis.cpp.
The renderer consumes the results in src/main.cpp and sends them to shader
uniforms.

### 1. Audio Mixdown

Input audio arrives as interleaved samples. The analyzer mixes it to mono by
averaging channels. This is correct for visualization because we want one set of
musical features, not separate left/right musical decisions.

Important limitation: if a source has phase-cancelled stereo content, mono
averaging can reduce some material. That is rare for the control signals we care
about, but if it becomes a problem we can switch to RMS channel combine instead
of arithmetic averaging.


### 2. Windowed FFT

The analyzer uses:

- Hann window
- KissFFT real FFT
- fftSize = 1024 by default
- magnitude spectrum, not complex phase

The Hann window reduces edge discontinuities before the FFT. The FFT gives us
frequency-bin magnitudes. Every later feature starts from this spectrum or from
the mono time-domain frame.

Tradeoff:

- 1024 samples is responsive enough for live input.
- Larger FFT sizes give better bass frequency resolution, but more latency and
  slower visual response.
- Smaller FFT sizes react faster, but bass bands become less stable.


### 3. Broadband Features

Current outputs:

- rms
- spectralCentroidHz
- spectralFlux
- spectralRolloff
- spectralFlatness
- spectralCrest

RMS is the root-mean-square loudness proxy for the current frame.

Spectral centroid is the "center of gravity" of the magnitude spectrum. Higher
centroid means brighter, sharper, more high-frequency-heavy sound. This is a
good shader input for color temperature, edge sharpness, bloom, or noise scale.

Spectral flux is the positive frame-to-frame magnitude increase summed over bins.
It is a novelty/onset-like signal: it rises when the spectrum changes. This is
more useful than volume alone because a new hi-hat or synth stab can be visible
as spectral movement even when total loudness barely changes.

Rolloff, flatness, and crest describe timbral shape:

- spectralRolloff is the normalized frequency below which 85% of spectral
  energy sits. It is a brightness/air control that complements centroid.
- spectralFlatness is geometric mean / arithmetic mean of the magnitude
  spectrum. It rises for noise-like material and falls for more tonal material.
- spectralCrest is a normalized max/mean ratio. It rises when the spectrum is
  peaky and sparse.

Renderer mapping:

- u_centroidNorm is normalized from spectralCentroidHz by Nyquist.
- u_flux is scaled and smoothed before reaching shaders.
- u_rolloff, u_flatness, and u_crest are already normalized 0..1 and smoothed in
  the renderer.


### 4. Log-Spaced Band Energies

Current outputs:

- bandEnergy[16]
- bandEnergyNorm[16]
- energyLow
- energyMid
- energyHigh

The analyzer builds 16 log-spaced bands between lowBandHz and highBandHz. Log
spacing is better than linear spacing for music because pitch and hearing are
closer to logarithmic than linear. Bass needs narrower bands; highs can be
grouped more broadly.

For each band, we average FFT magnitudes in the band. Then we normalize the band
against its own slow EMA baseline:

    ema = 0.995 * ema + 0.005 * (energy + epsilon)
    ratio = energy / max(ema, epsilon)
    normalized = clamp((ratio - 0.45) / 2.55, 0, 1)
    bandEnergyNorm = pow(normalized, 0.75)

This is a simple automatic gain control per band. It prevents loud tracks from
permanently saturating the visuals and quiet tracks from doing nothing.

Why the shaping matters:

- Raw ratio = energy / ema spikes too hard.
- The 0.45 offset ignores tiny changes around the baseline.
- The 2.55 scale gives headroom.
- The 0.75 power curve keeps musical movement visible without turning every
  tiny fluctuation into a full trigger.

The analyzer also collapses normalized bands into:

- low: center frequency below 250 Hz
- mid: 250 Hz to 4000 Hz
- high: above 4000 Hz

These are smoothed and become stable shader controls for bass, body, and air.


### 5. Per-Band Onsets

Current outputs:

- bandOnset[16]
- onsetStrength

For each band, the analyzer compares current energy to previous energy:

    inc = max(0, currentEnergy - previousEnergy)

Then it tracks a mean and variance-like EMA of that increase. A band onset is
reported when the current increase exceeds an adaptive threshold:

    mean = EMA(inc)
    variance = EMA((inc - mean)^2)
    threshold = onsetThresholdBase * (mean + onsetVarianceWeight * stddev)

This is important because fixed thresholds fail across songs. Dense drops, quiet
intros, and mastered loud tracks have different baselines. The adaptive threshold
lets each band calibrate itself.

Why this helps shaders:

- Bass onsets can drive scale/zoom.
- Mid onsets can drive geometric changes or rotation.
- High onsets can drive sparkle, glitch, particles, or edge detail.

Current limitation:

- There is no refractory window per band yet. Refractory logic exists for the
  beat detector, but individual bandOnset values can still be busy in noisy
  material. The renderer currently shapes onsets with a decaying envelope to
  make them more usable.


### 6. Beat Detection And BPM

Current outputs:

- beatTriggered
- beatEnvelope
- bpm
- beatPhase
- beatConfidence

The current beat detector uses the lowest bands as a kick proxy. It keeps a
short history of low-band normalized energy, computes mean and variance, and
fires only if:

- the low-band energy is above the adaptive threshold
- enough time has passed since the last beat

The refractory interval avoids double-triggering faster than beatMinIntervalSec.
When a beat fires, beatEnvelope is set to 1.0 and then decays over
beatEnvelopeHoldSec. The shader receives both:

- u_beat: binary event for the frame
- u_beatEnv: decaying pulse for visual motion

BPM is estimated from the median of recent inter-beat intervals after enough
beats have been detected.

This is intentionally simpler than a full beat tracker. Ellis's dynamic
programming beat tracker shows why better beat tracking uses two ideas at once:
local onset strength and continuity of inter-beat interval. Our current system
does local onset strength plus a recent-IBI median and exposes a confidence
estimate.

The continuous beat signals are:

- beatPhase: 0..1 ramp between expected beats
- beatConfidence: how stable recent inter-beat intervals are, based on recent
  IBI variance

Shaders can mix between a free-running BPM oscillator and beatPhase using
beatConfidence. This lets motion stay musical between hits without pretending to
know the groove when the detector is unstable.


### 7. Approximate HPSS

Current outputs:

- percussiveEnergy
- harmonicEnergy
- percussiveRatio

The analyzer keeps recent magnitude frames. It estimates:

- time median per bin
- frequency median around each bin

Then it derives rough percussive and harmonic energies from how the current
spectrum differs from those medians.

This is not a full production HPSS implementation, but it is useful for visuals.
Percussive material tends to be transient and broad in frequency. Harmonic
material tends to be sustained and structured in frequency. The percussiveRatio
helps shaders decide whether to behave like a rhythm visualizer or a flowing
harmony visualizer.

Shader use:

- high percussiveRatio: sharper cuts, pulses, glitches, impacts
- low percussiveRatio: smoother warps, fluid motion, harmonic color drift


### 8. Musical Structure And Momentum

Current outputs:

- energyDelta
- dropTrigger
- breakState
- buildUp
- layerChange
- isolatedHit
- onsetDensity
- lowOnsetDensity
- highOnsetDensity
- novelty

The analyzer smooths the low/mid/high band vector and compares it to the
previous smoothed vector. The Euclidean change becomes energyDelta.

Then it derives:

- breakState: confidence that the track is in a low-energy section
- dropTrigger: pulse when energy returns strongly after a break
- buildUp: sustained rising-energy confidence
- layerChange: pulse when the low/mid/high texture changes sharply
- isolatedHit: sparse transient hit in a not-too-dense section
- onsetDensity: short-term all-band transient density
- lowOnsetDensity: short-term bass/kick transient density
- highOnsetDensity: short-term high-frequency transient density
- novelty: richer section-change signal from energy movement, chroma movement,
  timbral changes, percussive/harmonic balance, and onset density

These features are closer to how people describe EDM arrangement:

- "the break"
- "the buildup"
- "the drop"
- "a new layer came in"
- "that one FX hit"

They are not exact semantic labels, but they are more useful than raw loudness
because they carry time context.


### 9. Spectral Contrast

Current outputs:

- spectralContrast[6]
- spectralContrastMean

The analyzer computes six octave-like subbands from 80 Hz to 5120 Hz. For each
subband, it sorts magnitudes, averages the top and bottom 8% of values, and
normalizes the peak-vs-valley difference:

    contrast = (peak - valley) / max(peak + valley, epsilon)

This is a lightweight real-time version of spectral contrast. It captures
whether a region of the spectrum is peaky/tonal or dense/noisy.

Shader use:

- high contrast: sharper ridges, hard edges, crystalline or articulated detail
- low contrast: foggier fluid motion, softer wash, less brittle geometry


### 10. Chroma And Harmonic Motion

Current outputs:

- chroma[12]
- chromaFlux

The analyzer maps FFT bins from roughly 55 Hz to 5000 Hz into the nearest MIDI
pitch class:

    midi = 69 + 12 * log2(frequency / 440)
    pitchClass = round(midi) mod 12

Each bin contributes sqrt(magnitude) to its pitch class, then the 12-bin vector
is normalized. chromaFlux is the frame-to-frame Euclidean distance between the
current chroma vector and the previous one.

This is not a chord detector. It is a compact harmonic color signal that is
good enough for palette relationships and hue motion.

Shader use:

- chroma drives palette blends and pitch-class color relationships
- chromaFlux drives harmonic color change and secondary motion
- stable chroma can keep palettes coherent during sustained sections


## How The Renderer Stabilizes The Data

The renderer does another pass of smoothing before sending values to GLSL.
This is where we fix the "too shaky" behavior.

The current approach uses attack/release followers:

- attack controls how quickly a value rises
- release controls how slowly it falls

This is better than one generic smoothing slider because music is asymmetric:
hits should arrive quickly, but visual energy should decay more slowly.

Current UI controls:

- Musical reactivity
- Band attack
- Band release
- Onset release

Current smoothed shader arrays:

- u_bands uses smoothedBands
- u_onsets uses smoothedOnsets

Current smoothed scalar uniforms:

- u_rms
- u_bandLow
- u_bandMid
- u_bandHigh
- u_onset
- u_flux
- u_energyDelta
- u_breakState
- u_buildUp
- u_rolloff
- u_flatness
- u_crest
- u_contrastMean
- u_chromaFlux
- u_onsetDensity
- u_lowDensity
- u_highDensity
- u_beatConfidence
- u_novelty

Additional smoothed shader arrays:

- u_contrastBands[6] uses smoothedContrastBands
- u_chroma[12] uses smoothedChroma

Some event-like uniforms still intentionally remain sharper:

- u_beat
- u_beatEnv
- u_drop
- u_layerChange
- u_isolatedHit
- u_beatPhase

Rule of thumb:

- Energy and texture should be smoothed.
- Onsets should be shaped into pulses.
- Beat should have both a hard event and a soft envelope.
- Section states should change slowly and avoid flicker.
- beatPhase should stay responsive, while beatConfidence controls how much a
  shader should trust it.


## Why Raw FFT Still Looks Bad By Itself

Raw band magnitudes are unstable shader inputs because they combine too many
different musical causes:

- louder master
- actual kick hit
- bass note sustain
- vocal consonant
- cymbal wash
- noise sweep
- reverb tail
- dense drop compression

All of those can move FFT bins, but they should not all create the same visual
reaction. The analyzer's job is to separate "amount of spectrum" from "musical
meaning of the change."

For visual beauty, we should avoid one-to-one mapping like:

    band magnitude -> displacement

Instead we want layered mapping:

    low band envelope -> scale/zoom/pressure
    low band onset -> kick impact
    high onset density -> sparkle/detail
    spectral centroid -> brightness/color temperature
    spectral contrast -> texture sharpness/depth
    chroma -> palette/hue relationships
    beat phase -> cyclic motion
    buildUp -> tension/ramp
    breakState -> calm/minimal mode
    dropTrigger -> large transition


## Research-Backed Descriptor Families

### 1. Spectral Rolloff, Flatness, And Crest

Tzanetakis and Cook use timbral texture features such as spectral centroid,
rolloff, flux, zero crossings, and MFCCs as compact descriptions of musical
audio. In this repo we compute centroid, flux, rolloff, flatness, and crest:

- spectralRolloff: frequency below which most spectral energy sits
- spectralFlatness: geometric mean / arithmetic mean of magnitude spectrum
- spectralCrest: max magnitude / average magnitude

Shader value:

- rolloff: brightness/air without relying only on centroid
- flatness: noise-like vs tonal material
- crest: peaky sparse spectrum vs dense spectrum

Implemented uniforms:

- u_rolloff
- u_flatness
- u_crest


### 2. Spectral Contrast

Jiang, Lu, Zhang, Tao, and Cai propose octave-based spectral contrast. Instead
of averaging a band, it compares strong spectral peaks and valleys inside each
octave band. This captures relative spectral structure: peaky harmonic material
versus flatter noisy material.

For our live visualizer, we do not need the paper's classifier stage. We can use
the raw contrast idea. The current implementation sorts each band and compares
the top and bottom 8% of magnitudes:

    sort magnitudes inside each octave/subband
    peak = mean(top 8%)
    valley = mean(bottom 8%)
    contrast = (peak - valley) / max(peak + valley, epsilon)

We use six octave-like bands. The result is normalized 0..1 and smoothed before
it reaches shaders.

Shader value:

- high contrast: sharp harmonic peaks, clean tonal focus, strong edges
- low contrast: noisy/dense/washy material, diffuse fog/fluid behavior

Implemented uniforms:

- u_contrastBands[6]
- u_contrastMean

Implementation note:

The exact max/min is intentionally avoided because a single FFT bin can be an
outlier.


### 3. Chroma / Pitch-Class Energy

Chroma features represent energy in the 12 pitch classes:

    C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, A#, B

Muller and Ewert describe chroma features as useful in MIR because they pool
frequencies that differ by octaves and therefore correlate with harmonic
progression while being more robust to instrumentation.

The repo uses a lightweight real-time version from the FFT:

    for each FFT bin in a musical range:
        frequency -> MIDI note number
        pitchClass = round(midi) mod 12
        chroma[pitchClass] += sqrt(magnitude)
    normalize chroma vector
    smooth over time

The implementation avoids very low sub-bass and extreme highs for this:

- start around 55 Hz
- stop around 5000 Hz

Shader value:

- palette hue from dominant pitch class
- palette blending from chroma vector
- harmonicMotion from frame-to-frame chroma distance
- calmer visuals when harmony is stable
- richer color changes when chords change

Implemented uniforms:

- u_chroma[12]
- u_chromaFlux

Implementation note:

This will be approximate because FFT-bin pitch mapping is not a full pitch
tracker. That is acceptable for color control. It should not be used for exact
chord naming.


### 4. Onset Density And Rhythmic Density

Single onsets create pulses, but music also has density:

- sparse breakdown
- steady four-on-the-floor kick
- busy hi-hat pattern
- noisy snare build

The repo tracks event rate over a short smoothing window:

- onsetDensity: all-band onset activity over the last 1 to 2 seconds
- lowOnsetDensity: kick/bass transient density
- highOnsetDensity: hats/noise density

Shader value:

- dense highs add fine detail or particle count
- dense lows add pressure and large-scale movement
- sparse sections simplify the image

Implemented uniforms:

- u_onsetDensity
- u_lowDensity
- u_highDensity


### 5. Beat Phase And Tempo Confidence

Binary beat triggers are not enough for beautiful motion. The analyzer exposes
a continuous phase:

    beatPhase = elapsedSinceLastBeat / estimatedInterBeatInterval

The phase is wrapped to 0..1. When BPM confidence is low, shaders should fade
toward a free-running oscillator or slow fallback. When confidence is high, they
can use beatPhase for cyclic motion that feels locked to the groove.

Shader value:

- rotating forms can complete one cycle per beat or bar
- waves can anticipate the next beat instead of jerking on impact
- transitions can happen on beat divisions

Implemented uniforms:

- u_beatPhase
- u_beatConfidence

Implementation note:

This should be tied to recent IBI stability. If recent beat intervals vary a
lot, confidence should fall so the shader does not pretend to know the groove.


### 6. Novelty / Section Change Curve

energyDelta is based on low/mid/high movement. novelty is a richer section
change cue. The current implementation combines low/mid/high energy movement
with spectral contrast movement, flatness movement, percussive-ratio movement,
chroma flux, and onset density:

    novelty = weighted distance of:
        low/mid/high energy movement
        chromaFlux
        spectralContrastMean change
        spectralFlatness change
        percussiveRatio change
        onsetDensity

Shader value:

- section changes can produce coherent visual transitions
- layer changes can be detected from timbre/harmony, not just volume

Implemented uniforms:

- u_novelty


## Practical Tuning Order

Do not add everything at once. Too many new uniforms without testing will make
shader behavior harder to tune.

The descriptor bus is now implemented. Recommended tuning order:

1. Tune spectral rolloff, flatness, crest, and spectral contrast in shaders.

   These help immediately with texture and brightness.

2. Tune chroma[12] and chromaFlux palette mapping.

   This gives the shaders harmonic color data without trying to name chords.

3. Tune onset density and beat phase.

   This improves groove and reduces the dependence on single-frame triggers.

4. Keep UI debug readouts visible while tuning.

   The data must be inspectable. If we cannot see the feature values, we cannot
   tune them.

5. Tune one or two shaders first before making the whole set more aggressive.

   Good candidates:

   - fluid_displacement_audio.frag: use contrast/flatness for texture behavior
   - code_sin.frag or rm_spheres.frag: use chroma/beatPhase for color/motion

6. Tune with real music capture.

   Use Pulse/PipeWire monitor input when visualizing desktop audio. The analysis
   only improves the shader if the input source is actually the mixed output or
   the intended music stream.

## Testing The Analyzer

We need tooling outside the repo or isolated from production code so analysis can
be tested without manually watching the window.

Minimum useful tooling:

1. A CLI feature dump tool

   Input:

   - WAV file or generated test signal

   Output:

   - CSV rows with time and feature values

   This lets us inspect whether bands, onsets, beat, contrast, chroma, and
   density behave correctly over time.

2. Generated test signals

   Useful tests:

   - silence: all features should settle near zero
   - sine at 110 Hz: low band and one chroma class should dominate
   - sine sweep: centroid and rolloff should rise
   - impulse train at 120 BPM: beat detector and beatPhase should lock
   - white noise burst: flatness should rise, chroma should stay diffuse
   - kick-like low pulse plus high hats: low and high onset densities should
     separate

3. Feature plots

   A small script can plot CSV columns. This is the fastest way to see whether
   a feature is useful before wiring it to a shader.

4. Shader uniform debug panel

   The ImGui panel should expose current values for the important features. The
   UI must use stable labels and unique IDs so duplicate ImGui IDs do not hide or
   corrupt controls.


## Key Design Rules

1. Do not use raw FFT bins directly for large visual movement.

Use smoothed, normalized, musically meaningful features.

2. Separate events from states.

A kick is an event. A breakdown is a state. A buildup is a trend. They need
different smoothing and different shader behavior.

3. Use attack/release instead of one generic smoothing amount.

Visuals should rise quickly on musical impacts and decay slowly enough to feel
connected.

4. Prefer feature families over single magic numbers.

Better:

    bass envelope + bass onset + beat phase + contrast

Worse:

    FFT bin 3 controls everything

5. Keep shader inputs normalized.

The shader should not need to know about sample rate, FFT size, or raw magnitude
scale.

6. Add features only when we can observe them.

Every new descriptor should have a debug value or CSV output so we can tune it
with real songs.


## Source Notes

The current direction is based on these MIR/audio-analysis references:

- George Tzanetakis and Perry Cook, "Musical Genre Classification of Audio
  Signals", IEEE Transactions on Speech and Audio Processing, 2002.
  Useful for the timbre/rhythm/pitch feature split and features such as
  centroid, rolloff, flux, and rhythm histograms.
  https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~gtzan/work/pubs/tsap02gtzan.pdf

- Daniel P. W. Ellis, "Beat Tracking by Dynamic Programming", Journal of New
  Music Research, 2007.
  Useful for the idea that beat tracking should combine local onset strength
  with continuity of inter-beat interval, rather than trusting isolated peaks.
  https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/pubs/Ellis07-beattrack.pdf
  https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/LabROSA/matlab/beat_simple/

- Dan-Ning Jiang, Lie Lu, Hong-Jiang Zhang, Jian-Hua Tao, and Lian-Hong Cai,
  "Music Type Classification by Spectral Contrast Feature", 2002.
  Useful for spectral contrast: octave/subband peak-vs-valley structure as a
  better timbre descriptor than average band energy alone.
  https://hcsi.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/Paper/Paper02/200218.pdf

- Meinard Muller and Sebastian Ewert, Chroma Toolbox resources, ISMIR 2011.
  Useful for chroma/pitch-class features and the reason 12-bin chroma is a good
  harmony/color descriptor.
  https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/chromatoolbox/

- Olivier Lartillot and Petri Toiviainen, "A Toolbox for Musical Feature
  Extraction from Audio", ISMIR 2007.
  Useful as a broader feature taxonomy: RMS, envelope, onset, tempo, spectral
  flux, centroid, flatness, rolloff, chromagram, and segmentation features are
  all part of the same MIR feature-building approach.
  https://ismir2007.ismir.net/proceedings/ISMIR2007_p127_lartillot.pdf

- J. P. Bello, L. Daudet, S. Abdallah, C. Duxbury, M. Davies, and M. B.
  Sandler, "A Tutorial on Onset Detection in Music Signals", IEEE Transactions
  on Speech and Audio Processing, 2005. DOI: 10.1109/TSA.2005.851998.
  Useful background for onset detection as a staged process: time-frequency
  processing, onset/novelty function extraction, and peak/event selection.
